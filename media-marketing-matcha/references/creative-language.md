# Creative language

Creativity here means **precision that surprises**, not decoration. A line is
good when it says the thing more exactly than the plain version did, and the
extra exactness is what makes it memorable. If a phrase is only pretty, it's
drag.

Always offer several options and let the writer choose. Different hooks imply
different pieces; seeing three makes the choice visible.

## Hooks

A hook earns one more sentence. That is all it has to do. It does not have to
summarize, promise, or impress.

**The concrete detail.** Start inside a specific moment, no setup.
> The invoice was for $14. The meeting about the invoice cost about $600.

**The admission.** Say the thing people usually don't.
> I've been running standups for six years and I still don't know if they help.

**The reversal.** Set up the expected shape, then break it, only if the setup
is a belief people genuinely hold.
> Everyone told me hiring senior people would slow us down. It did. That turned
> out to be the point.

**The overheard line.** A quoted fragment drops the reader mid-scene.
> "Can you just make it look like the mockup?"

**The odd number.** Unrounded numbers read as real.
> We rewrote the onboarding flow 4 times. The version that worked was closest to
> the first one.

**The small stake.** Deliberately low drama, which is itself disarming amid
posts about transformation.
> This is a post about a dropdown menu.

**The definition move.** Name a thing everyone has experienced but not labeled.
> There's a specific kind of tired that comes from work that went fine.

**The question you actually can't answer.** Only if you truly can't.
> Why does every tool that promises to reduce meetings end up generating
> meetings?

Avoid: rhetorical questions with obvious answers, "Picture this:", "Let me tell
you about the time...", any hook that describes the post instead of starting it.

## Memorable phrases

Devices that make a line stick. Reach for these when a good idea is sitting in
flat language:

- **Compression.** Say it in fewer words than feels comfortable. *"We shipped
  faster because we argued earlier."*
- **Unexpected noun pairing.** Two words that don't usually meet. *"meeting
  debt," "polite backlog," "decision fog."*
- **The reframe.** Rename the thing so its nature changes. *"That's not a
  roadmap, it's a wish list with dates."*
- **Antithesis.** Two halves in tension, balanced. *"Easy to start, expensive to
  finish."*
- **Concrete metaphor from a non-business domain.** Kitchens, weather,
  carpentry, sport, gardening. Business metaphors ("runway," "north star") are
  worn out; a metaphor from cooking still has texture. Use one per piece. More
  than that becomes trying too hard.
- **The specific stand-in.** Replace a category with one true instance. Not "our
  processes," but "the spreadsheet Maya maintains by hand every Friday."
- **Understatement.** Let the reader feel the size of it. *"It was, in
  retrospect, an ambitious deadline."*

Test each phrase by reading it out loud. If it would embarrass the writer to say
it in a normal conversation, it fails.

## Transitions

Weak drafts leak energy between paragraphs. Transitions worth using:

- **The hinge sentence:** one short line that turns the piece. *"Then the
  numbers came back."*
- **The echo:** repeat a word from the previous paragraph in a new sense.
- **The concession:** *"Which is the part I got wrong."* Concessions build
  trust and move the argument at the same time.
- **The zoom:** from the specific incident to what it's an instance of, or back
  down. Alternating scale is what makes essays feel like they're going
  somewhere.

Don't use: "Moreover," "Furthermore," "That said" as a reflex, or a numbered
list where a sentence would do.

## Headlines

Give at least three, deliberately different in strategy:

1. **Plain and specific:** says what it is, trusts the subject. *"What six
   years of standups taught me about standups."*
2. **Curiosity through tension:** an unresolved pair. *"The cheapest fix was
   the one nobody would approve."*
3. **The phrase-forward:** leads with the piece's best coinage. *"Meeting
   debt."*

Good headlines are usually **shorter than feels safe** and **more specific than
feels safe**. Colon-splitting ("X: How to Y") is a warning sign; it's usually
two weak halves stapled together.

## Ending lines

The ending is where most drafts collapse, either into a summary of what was
just read, or into a moral. Both are exits, not landings. Better options:

- **The turn inward.** End on something the writer is still figuring out.
- **The callback.** Return to the opening image with new meaning. Reliable and
  satisfying because the reader gets to feel the distance travelled.
- **The narrowed claim.** Resist the urge to universalize. *"I don't know if
  this generalizes. It was true for us."*
- **The unresolved beat.** Stop one sentence before the moral. The reader
  supplies it, and thereby owns it.
- **The plain last fact.** Just what happened, no commentary. Often the
  strongest.

Never end with: a summary of the piece, a call to engage, a question aimed at
the algorithm, or an emoji-punctuated exhortation.

## Working the options

When generating a set: vary the *strategy*, not just the wording. Three hooks
that are all "concrete detail" with different nouns is one option presented
three times. Three hooks that are admission / reversal / small stake give the
writer a real choice about what the piece is.

Label each option with what it's doing ("admission," "reversal") so the writer
learns the moves and can do it themselves next time. That is a better outcome
than dependence.
