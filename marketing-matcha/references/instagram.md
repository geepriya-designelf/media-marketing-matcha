# Instagram

## The translation problem

Instagram is not a shorter LinkedIn. It's a different reading posture: the
reader is scrolling fast, half-attending, and deciding in under a second whether
to stop. So the thought has to arrive **visually and rhythmically** before it
arrives logically.

The constraint that matters: the same underlying thought survives, but it gets
delivered in fragments the reader assembles. Make it punchier and more visual —
never dumber. A simplified idea and a compressed idea are different things.
Compression keeps the edges.

## First-slide hook

The first slide is the whole battle. It should be readable in one glance from a
distance — think 6 to 10 words, large.

What works:

- **A phrase, not a sentence.** *"Meeting debt."*
- **A short admission.** *"I ran standups for 6 years."*
- **A specific number.** *"$14 invoice. $600 meeting."*
- **A flat statement with tension in it.** *"The dropdown took four months."*

What doesn't: full sentences that require squinting, questions with obvious
answers, anything that reads as a title rather than a beginning.

## Carousel concept

Before outlining slides, name the *concept* — the organizing idea that gives the
carousel a shape. A carousel without a concept is a blog post cut into pieces,
and it reads that way.

Useful concepts:

- **The unfolding** — each slide reveals one more piece of a situation; the
  reader keeps swiping to find out what happened.
- **The before / after pair-up** — alternating slides, same structure, changing
  content.
- **The list that earns itself** — only if each item is genuinely surprising.
  Weakest option; use when the content really is enumerable.
- **The single idea, turned** — 5-6 slides examining one thought from different
  angles, ending somewhere the first slide didn't predict.
- **The annotated artifact** — a screenshot, a receipt, a calendar, a diagram,
  broken down slide by slide. Strong when the writer has a real artifact.

## Slide-by-slide outline

Give an actual outline, one line per slide, with a note on what's visually on
it. Six to eight slides is the honest range; ten is usually two carousels
wearing a trenchcoat.

Density rule: **one idea per slide, 15-25 words maximum.** If a slide needs more
than that, it's two slides or it belongs in the caption. Slides that are dense
get skipped, and a skipped slide breaks the chain — the reader leaves.

Include a note on visual treatment where it matters: which words are large,
whether a slide is mostly white space, whether one slide should be a single word
for rhythm. Rhythm across slides matters as much as within them — a very short
slide after two dense ones resets attention.

The last slide should land the thought, not ask for a follow. If the idea is
good, the follow is a consequence.

## Caption

The caption is where the writer's actual voice lives — the slides are the hook,
the caption is the conversation. It can be longer and looser than the slides:
2-4 short paragraphs, first line doing real work since Instagram truncates
early.

The caption should **add**, not repeat. If it restates the carousel, there was
no reason to read it. Good captions give the backstory the slides couldn't hold,
or the honest caveat, or what happened after.

Hashtags: a small set of genuinely relevant ones at the end, or none. Blocks of
30 are noise and read as trying too hard.

## Memorable closing line

One line the reader could screenshot. It should work on its own, out of context
— which is the real test of whether the thought was ever sharp.

Avoid: "Save this for later," "Share with someone who needs this," "Follow for
more." These convert the piece into an ad for itself.

## When Instagram isn't the right home

Say so if the idea genuinely doesn't translate — some arguments need connective
tissue that slides can't carry, and a bad carousel costs more than no carousel.
When that happens, offer the alternative: a single-image quote card with a
strong caption, or a story-format walkthrough. Recommending against a format is
useful advice, not a failure to deliver.
