# LinkedIn

## What LinkedIn actually is for this person

The goal is not maximum likes. The goal is that the right people in their
network stop, recognize the idea, and want to respond. Those are different
targets and they pull in opposite directions — broad reach usually means broad
phrasing, and broad phrasing is exactly what stops a specific person from
recognizing themselves in it.

So write for recognition, not for reach. The measure of a good post here is a
reply that says "this is exactly the thing I couldn't name last week."

## Finding the angle

A blog post usually contains three or four ideas. A LinkedIn post can carry one.
Choosing which one is the actual work — most of the value you provide is in this
choice, not in the writing.

Pick the idea that:

- **A specific person could have lived.** Abstract insight is scrollable; a
  recognizable situation is not.
- **Has a natural counterparty.** Someone who'd say "yes, and" or "no, because."
  If nobody could reasonably disagree, there's no conversation.
- **Doesn't need the blog to make sense.** The post has to stand alone. Linking
  the blog is a bonus, not a dependency.
- **Costs the writer a little something to say.** A mild admission, a position
  they'd have to defend. That cost is what reads as real.

Name the angle explicitly when you present it: "The angle I'd take is X, aimed
at Y, because Z." Then give the alternatives you rejected in one line each — it
shows the choice was made, not defaulted into.

## Structure that works without gimmicks

LinkedIn truncates around the third line, so the first two lines matter
mechanically. That's a real constraint, not a reason to write bait.

A shape that holds up:

1. **Opening line** — a specific situation or admission. No preamble.
2. **One or two lines that raise the tension** before the reader hits "see more."
3. **The middle** — what happened, or what you noticed. Concrete. This is where
   the piece earns the reader's time.
4. **The turn** — the thing that makes it worth having read.
5. **The close** — a narrowed claim or an honest open question. Not a CTA.

Length: 120-250 words is the honest range for most ideas. Padding to hit a
"long post performs better" heuristic makes writing worse. If the thought is 90
words, let it be 90 words.

Formatting: white space between short paragraphs is fine and aids reading on
mobile. What to avoid is the every-line-is-a-paragraph staccato — it makes
ordinary sentences pretend to be aphorisms, and it's the most recognizable
influencer tell there is.

No emoji bullets. No "👇". No hashtag stacks — two or three genuinely relevant
ones at most, or none.

## What to deliver

Give the user all five, in this order:

- **The best LinkedIn angle** — one paragraph. Say who it's aimed at and why
  this angle over the others.
- **3 possible hooks** — different strategies, not three phrasings of one. Label
  what each is doing.
- **3 memorable phrases** — lines they could carry into a conversation, a talk,
  or a future post. These outlive the post itself.
- **A suggested LinkedIn post** — complete and ready to paste, in their voice.
- **2-3 natural conversation starters** — see below.

## Conversation starters

Not "Agree?" and not "What do you think?" Those ask the reader to do the work of
inventing a response.

A real conversation starter gives them a specific, low-cost thing to contribute:

- Asks for an instance, not an opinion: *"I'm curious whether anyone's seen this
  work at a bigger company — I've only tried it at ten people."*
- Names the counter-position honestly: *"The obvious objection is that this only
  works when the team already trusts each other. I don't have a good answer to
  that."*
- Admits a gap the reader might fill: *"I still don't know what to call this
  category of problem."*

Each of these works because it makes replying easy and makes the reply feel
useful rather than performative. Offer them as options the writer can drop into
the post or leave in the comments.
