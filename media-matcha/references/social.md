# Social

One post, both places. LinkedIn and Instagram are not treated as separate
deliverables here, the author posts the same thing to both, so the job is to
find the single version that survives both surfaces rather than two versions
that each optimise for one.

That constraint is useful, not limiting. A post that works as plain text *and*
as an image is a post whose idea is actually sharp; anything that needs a
platform's affordances to land was probably soft to begin with.

## What social is for here

Not maximum likes. The goal is that the right people stop, recognise the idea,
and want to respond. Those pull in opposite directions, broad reach means broad
phrasing, and broad phrasing is exactly what stops a specific person from
recognising themselves in it.

Write for recognition, not reach. The measure of a good post is a reply that
says "this is the thing I couldn't name last week."

## Finding the angle

A blog post usually holds three or four ideas. A social post carries one.
Choosing which one is the actual work, most of the value here is in the choice,
not the writing.

Pick the idea that:

- **A specific person could have lived.** Abstract insight is scrollable; a
  recognisable situation is not.
- **Has a natural counterparty.** Someone who'd say "yes, and" or "no, because."
  If nobody could reasonably disagree, there's no conversation.
- **Stands alone.** It can't depend on the blog to make sense. Linking the blog
  is a bonus.
- **Costs the writer a little something.** A mild admission, a position they'd
  have to defend. That cost is what reads as real.

Name the angle explicitly: "The angle I'd take is X, aimed at Y, because Z."
Then give the rejected alternatives in one line each, it shows the choice was
made rather than defaulted into.

## The first two lines

Both surfaces truncate early, LinkedIn around the third line, Instagram's
caption around the second. Same rule, so write to the stricter one: **the first
two lines have to earn the tap.**

That's a mechanical constraint, not a licence to write bait. A specific
situation or an admission does it. A tease ("I learned something wild this
week…") does not.

## Structure

1. **Opening line:** a specific situation or admission. No preamble.
2. **One or two lines that raise the tension** before the cut.
3. **The middle:** what happened, or what was noticed. Concrete.
4. **The turn:** the thing that makes it worth having read.
5. **The close:** a narrowed claim or an honest open question. Not a CTA.

**Length:** 120 to 250 words for most ideas. Padding toward "long posts perform
better" makes writing worse. If the thought is 90 words, let it be 90 words.

**Formatting:** white space between short paragraphs is fine and helps on
mobile. Avoid the every-line-is-a-paragraph staccato, it makes ordinary
sentences pretend to be aphorisms, and it's the most recognisable influencer
tell there is.

No emoji bullets. No "👇". Two or three genuinely relevant hashtags at most, or
none, blocks of thirty are noise on either platform.

## The visual

Instagram needs an image; LinkedIn doesn't but is helped by one. Since it's the
same post in both places, the visual should carry the *same* idea rather than
decorate it.

**Best case: the post itself is an artifact.** A receipt, a standup log, a job
description, an org chart, a changelog, a two-column comparison. These read as
an image on Instagram and paste as text on LinkedIn without losing anything,
one piece of writing, two surfaces, no translation. Reach for this first.

**Otherwise: a first-frame line.** Six to ten words, large, readable at a
glance from a distance. A phrase, not a sentence. A specific number. A flat
statement with tension in it. What fails: full sentences that need squinting,
questions with obvious answers, anything that reads as a title rather than a
beginning.

**If it wants to be a carousel**, say so and outline it, but only when the idea
genuinely has stages. Otherwise one strong frame beats seven weak ones.

- Name the concept first (the unfolding, the annotated artifact, the two-column,
  the single idea turned). A carousel without a concept is a post cut into
  pieces and it reads that way.
- One idea per slide, 15 to 25 words maximum. Dense slides get skipped, and a
  skipped slide breaks the chain.
- Six to eight slides. Ten is usually two carousels wearing a trenchcoat.
- Vary the rhythm, a very short slide after two dense ones resets attention.
- The last slide lands the thought. It does not ask for a follow.

## What to deliver

- **The angle:** one paragraph. Who it's aimed at, and why this angle over the
  others you considered.
- **3 hooks:** different strategies, not three phrasings of one. Label what
  each is doing.
- **3 memorable phrases:** lines they could carry into a conversation, a talk,
  or a future post. These outlive the post.
- **The post:** complete, ready to paste, in their voice.
- **The visual:** the artifact, or the first-frame line, or the carousel
  outline if it earns one.
- **2 to 3 conversation starters.**

## Conversation starters

Not "Agree?" and not "What do you think?" Those ask the reader to invent a
response from nothing.

A real one gives them something specific and low-cost to contribute:

- **Asks for an instance, not an opinion.** *"Curious whether anyone's seen this
  work at a bigger company, I've only tried it at ten people."*
- **Names the counter-position honestly.** *"The obvious objection is that this
  only works when the team already trusts each other. I don't have a good answer
  to that."*
- **Admits a gap the reader might fill.** *"I still don't know what to call this
  category of problem."*

Each works because replying is easy and the reply feels useful rather than
performative. Offer them as options, in the post, or held back for the
comments.

## When it doesn't translate

Say so if the idea genuinely doesn't survive the trip. Some arguments need
connective tissue a short post can't carry, and a bad post costs more than no
post. Recommending against publishing something is useful advice, not a failure
to deliver.
